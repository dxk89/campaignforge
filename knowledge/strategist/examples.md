# Worked examples: Strategist

Each example gives the inputs in brief, the decision, and the annotation. Read the annotations; they are the training.

---

## Example 1 — the obvious angle rejected for lack of proof

**Inputs.** Ledgerline, reconciliation for finance teams at 50–500 person SaaS companies. Objective: trial signups. Tone: direct. Context: proof points are a customer survey ("close four days faster on average"), connector coverage, SOC 2. Gaps: no named customers, no pricing comparison. Audience research: pain is the last week of the month; objection is "we already have Xero".

**Angles chosen.**
1. *Four days back* (problem-aware) — the measured time saved at month end.
2. *Sits in front of your ledger* (solution-aware) — positioning against the replacement fear.
3. *Exceptions only* (problem-aware, different entry point) — you review a short list, not everything.

**Lead: Four days back.** Reasoning given: trial signups respond to a concrete outcome, and this is the only number the context can prove. "Sits in front of your ledger" is the objection-handler and belongs in email 3, not the lead. What is given up: the positioning angle would build a clearer category story over a year, and this campaign does not buy that.

**Annotation.**
- A fourth angle was drafted and killed: *"The finance tool your auditor will thank you for."* It failed the provable test. SOC 2 is in the context; auditor gratitude is not. A junior keeps it because it sounds like a campaign.
- The three that survived sit at different points: two problem-aware but on different entry points (time lost, review burden), one solution-aware. Three phrasings of "save time" would have been one angle.
- The lead reasoning names the trade-off. A lead reasoning that only sells the choice is a red flag: it means the alternatives were never real.

**What a junior would have written.** "Angle 1: Save time. Angle 2: Save more time. Angle 3: Be more efficient." Lead: angle 1, reasoning: "it is the strongest benefit."

## Example 2 — brand awareness, no in-market angle

**Inputs.** Fictional client: Northsea Freight Data, market intelligence for shipping analysts. Objective: brand_awareness. Budget stated in campaign facts. Context: strong editorial credibility, no product numbers approved. Audience: analysts who change data providers roughly every four years.

**Angles.**
1. *The number nobody publishes* (unaware) — attached to the entry point "we could not source that figure anywhere".
2. *Between the shipping report and the trading desk* (solution-aware) — the gap the category leaves.
3. *Four years is a long time to live with the wrong feed* (problem-aware) — attached to the renewal entry point.

**Lead: The number nobody publishes.** Reasoning: with 95% of the audience out-market on a four-year cycle, the job is to attach the brand to the situation that will bring them into the category, and the situation this audience meets weekly is a figure they cannot source. Given up: the renewal angle would convert faster among the few in-market now, and the objective is not conversion.

**Annotation.**
- Note what changed with the objective. The same company, on a trial-signup brief, would lead with the renewal angle: it speaks to the in-market minority and asks for an action.
- The entry points are situations in the reader's week, not features of the product. "We could not source that figure" is a moment. "Comprehensive coverage" is not.
- No proof points were available, so every angle is capability-led or situation-led and no number appears. The gaps list is quoted in the reasoning so the writer downstream does not go looking.

**What a junior would have written.** An angle built on "trusted by leading analysts", which the context does not support, and a lead chosen because awareness campaigns "should be emotional".

## Example 3 — a learning overrides the brief's instinct

**Inputs.** Ledgerline, second campaign. Brief asks for a provocative tone. Learnings from campaign one: proof-led LinkedIn variants out-converted pain-led by roughly a third over 2,100 clicks; the client's voice observations say they avoid hype and use "exceptions", never "errors".

**Decision.** Angles remain provocative in *point of view*, not in register. The lead angle is a proof-led contrast: what the close looks like when the matching is already done. `lead_reasoning` states the conflict: the brief asked for provocative, the evidence says proof-led wins with this audience, so the provocation is in the claim, not the language.

**Annotation.**
- The learning is not silently obeyed and not silently ignored. It is surfaced in the reasoning so a person can overrule it.
- Tone and angle are separate decisions. A junior collapses them and writes shouty copy because the brief said provocative.
- The voice rules constrain the words available; the learning constrains the structure. Both bind.

**What a junior would have written.** Provocative headlines using "errors" and a competitor name, ignoring both the lexicon and the result.

## Self-check applied to these examples

For each: three angles at different stages or entry points? Lead names what was given up? Hooks execute the lead only? Nothing from gaps used? Learnings addressed rather than ignored? All three pass. Example 1's killed fourth angle is the visible evidence of the provable test being applied.
