# Worked examples: Copywriter

Original assets, annotated with the decision behind each and the junior alternative. Character counts shown as the writer must count them.

---

## Example 1 — Google RSA headlines, written first

**Inputs.** Ledgerline. Lead angle: *Four days back*. Approved claims: four days faster (customer survey 2026); Stripe, Adyen, GoCardless and 2,400 banks; SOC 2 Type II. Ad group: "payout reconciliation", terms include "reconcile stripe payouts", "payout matching tool".

**Headlines** (limit 30):

| # | Headline | Chars | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Close the Month 4 Days Faster | 29 | The claim, as a complete line. Works in any position |
| 2 | Ledgerline Payout Matching | 26 | Product name + group term. Name appears twice across the set |
| 3 | Reconcile Stripe Payouts | 24 | Direct keyword match for the group's strongest term |
| 4 | Review Exceptions, Not Lines | 28 | The mechanism, in the client's own word ("exceptions") |
| 5 | Free 14-Day Trial, No Card | 26 | The offer, stated once |
| 6 | Works With Xero and Sage | 24 | Compatibility, the top objection, as a fact not a claim |
| 7 | Ledgerline for Finance Teams | 28 | Product name + audience |
| 8 | 2,400 Banks and 3 Processors | 28 | Coverage as a number, from an approved claim |

**Annotation.**
- Written before Meta and LinkedIn. Thirty characters forces the campaign's ideas into their shortest true form, and the rest of the copy inherits that discipline.
- Every headline is a complete line. None depends on another, because Google may show any three in any order.
- The product name is in two, a group term in three. That is the rule, checked before submit.
- Headline 6 names compatible products, not competitors. Naming Xero as a compatibility fact is different from naming a competitor as a comparison, which the client's rules forbid.
- No headline uses "errors". The lexicon says exceptions.

**Junior alternative.** "The Best Reconciliation Tool" (unprovable superlative, no keyword), "Save Time and Money" (two ideas, no specificity), "Try Ledgerline Today →" (arrow, and a CTA where a claim should be), plus a headline that only makes sense after another one.

## Example 2 — LinkedIn intro, first line standing alone

**Asset.** Intro text (soft target ~150):

> Your finance team loses about a week a month to payout matching. Ledgerline customers get four of those days back.

**Chars.** 121.

**Annotation.**
- The first sentence is the ad. Everything after "see more" is a bonus, so the pain and its size are in the first eleven words.
- "About a week" is a characterisation of the audience research, not a claim about the product, so it needs no approved claim. "Four of those days" is the approved claim and carries the source.
- No greeting, no "Are you struggling with…", no company introduction. LinkedIn's feed punishes brand throat-clearing, and the format's CTR benchmarks are unforgiving of it.
- Variant 2 for the same campaign leads with the mechanism and variant 3 with compatibility. Same angle, different hook and proof. That is what "variants" means.

**Junior alternative.** "At Ledgerline, we believe finance teams deserve better. That's why we built a revolutionary AI-powered reconciliation platform…" — brand first, banned words, claim with no source, and the reader has already scrolled.

## Example 3 — email 2, one job and one CTA

**Asset.** Subject: *What "4 days faster" looks like in practice* (44). Preview: *A close that starts with 23 exceptions, not 4,100 lines.* (56)

Body, abridged to show structure:

> Yesterday I mentioned that Ledgerline customers close the month four days faster. Here is what that looks like.
>
> On the 1st, last month's payouts are already matched. The finance lead opens Ledgerline and sees 23 exceptions out of 4,100 transactions: a refund split across two payouts, a bank fee posted late, a duplicate invoice. Each takes a minute or two.
>
> By the afternoon of the 1st, reconciliation is done and the close moves on to accruals.
>
> The trial connects to your real accounts, so you see your own exception list.
>
> See your exceptions

**Annotation.**
- One job: prove the claim made in email 1. Not to introduce, not to ask for the sale. Email 3 does the asking.
- One CTA, at the end, phrased as what the reader gets rather than what we want.
- The numbers in the walkthrough (23 of 4,100) are illustrative and framed as a scenario, not as a customer result. Had they been framed as a customer's actual figures they would have needed an approved claim. This distinction is the one juniors get wrong most often.
- The subject is specific rather than curious. Curiosity and specificity both work; using both in one line produces neither.
- The click on "See your exceptions" is the branch signal the lifecycle uses. The copy and the workflow are designed together.

**Junior alternative.** Three links (case study, pricing, demo), a subject line of eleven words, and a body that re-introduces the product because "some readers may have missed email 1".

## Example 4 — no proof points available

**Inputs.** A client whose context has an empty `proof_points` list and a gap noted: "no customer numbers".

**Meta primary text.**

> Matching payouts to invoices by hand is a week you do not get back. Ledgerline does the matching overnight and gives you the exceptions.

**Annotation.**
- Capability-led. It says what the product does and what that means, and asserts nothing about results.
- Nothing was invented to fill the space. The temptation to write "join hundreds of finance teams" is exactly what the claims gate exists to stop.
- The gap is the client's problem to fix, and the research pass has said so. The copy's job is to be good without it.

**Junior alternative.** "Trusted by finance teams everywhere." Unprovable, unspecific, and it would be flagged as an unapproved claim.

## Self-check applied

Each asset above: executes the lead angle; every number traces to an approved claim or is framed as illustrative; first lines stand alone; RSA headlines combine in any order; one CTA per email; counts within limits; no banned words; the client's lexicon respected.
