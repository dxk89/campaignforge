# Long-form that earns its length

## Conclusion first

The pyramid principle: state the conclusion, then support it. B2B readers scan; a piece that builds to its point loses the people who would have agreed with it. The first paragraph says what this piece argues and what the reader should do differently.

## What makes a thought-leadership piece

Four things, all required:

1. **One arguable claim.** If nobody in the industry would disagree, it is not a point of view, it is a summary.
2. **Evidence.** Approved claims, the client's own experience, or published sources cited properly. Experience is the strongest and the most underused: what the client has actually seen is the part no competitor can copy.
3. **A counter-argument, handled.** The strongest version of the opposing case, answered. Ignoring it is what makes thought leadership read as marketing.
4. **A practical implication.** What the reader does on Monday.

## Case studies

Situation, complication, resolution, result. The complication is the part juniors skip and the part readers come for: what went wrong, what was tried first, why it did not work. A case study with no complication is a testimonial with paragraphs.

Every number in a case study is an approved claim with a source. Customer names need permission, which means published permission, not assumed.

## Style

- Sentences that carry one idea. Vary the length; three long sentences in a row lose the thread.
- Concrete nouns. "The reconciliation" not "the process".
- No throat-clearing openings. "In today's fast-paced business environment" is a sentence that means the writer had not started yet.
- Readability targeted at the audience, not at a grade level for its own sake: technical readers tolerate complexity in the subject, not in the syntax.
- Subheadings that say something. "Introduction" and "Conclusion" are labels, not signposts.
